\section{Electromagnetic potentials}

We have established that a conservative force that depends on position can be
written using a scalar function
\[
	F = - \grad U.
\]
Now we ask if the same construction can fully describe the electromagnetic force.

The complete electromagnetic force is given by the Lorentz force
\[
	F = q (E + v \times B).
\]
While the electrostatic contribution depends only on position, the magnetic
contribution depends explicitly on the velocity of the charged particle:
\[
	F_B = q v \times B,
\]
and therefore cannot be obtained from the gradient of a potential energy depending
only on position. A scalar potential alone is no longer sufficient.
A useful consequence of this velocity dependence is that the magnetic force
does no work on the particle. Indeed,
\[
	F_B \cdot v
	=
	q(v \times B)\cdot v
	=
	0,
\]
because \(v \times B\) is perpendicular to \(v\).
Therefore, a magnetic field may change the direction of the particle's
velocity, but not its magnitude or kinetic energy. This means that the magnetic
field requires a different kind of potential.
\subsection{Mathematical interlude: the divergence and curl operators}

The divergence of a vector field $\vec{F}=(F_x,F_y,F_z)$ is defined as the dot product:
\[
	\nabla \cdot \vec{F}
	=
	\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}
	+
	\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y}
	+
	\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}.
\]
It returns a scalar. It measures how much the field locally ``flows out of'' or ``flows into'' a point. If the divergence is positive, the point behaves like a source; if it is negative, like a sink; if it is zero, there is no net local outgoing flux.


The curl of $\vec{F}$ is defined as the cross product:
\[
	\nabla \times \vec{F}
	=
	\left(
	\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial y}
	-
	\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial z},
	\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial z}
	-
	\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial x},
	\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x}
	-
	\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}
	\right).
\]
Notice how the same rule for the cross product between two vectors was used:
\[
	(a,b,c)\times(d,e,f)
	=
	(bf-ce,\,cd-af,\,ae-bd),
\]
and considering:
\[
	\vec{F}=(F_x,F_y,F_z),
	\qquad
	\nabla
	=
	\left(
	\frac{\partial}{\partial x},
	\frac{\partial}{\partial y},
	\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
	\right).
\]
It returns a vector and measures the local tendency of the field to generate a rotation. In other words, the curl tells us whether, in an infinitesimal neighbourhood of a point, the field tends to make a small object placed there rotate.

Divergence and curl therefore describe two different aspects of a vector field: divergence measures its ``radial'' behaviour, that is, how much the field locally expands or contracts; curl instead measures its ``circular'' behaviour, that is, how much the field tends to rotate. This distinction is illustrated in \cref{fig:divergence-curl}.


\subsection{The magnetic vector potential}
One of Maxwell's equations states that the magnetic field is divergence-free:
\[
	\nabla \cdot B = 0.
\]
In classical electromagnetism, this expresses the absence of isolated
magnetic sources.

A standard result of vector calculus states that a sufficiently regular
divergence-free vector field can be expressed, at least locally, as the
curl of another vector field. We may therefore introduce a vector field
\(A(r,t)\) such that
\[
	B = \nabla \times A.
\]
The field \(A\) is called the magnetic vector potential. Unlike the scalar
potential introduced previously, \(A\) does not represent a potential
energy per unit charge.

To understand its relation to the magnetic field, consider a closed curve
\(C\). At each point along the curve, the infinitesimal displacement
\(dr\) is tangent to the path. The quantity
\[
	A \cdot dr
\]
measures the component of \(A\) along the path, multiplied by the
infinitesimal distance travelled. Its integral around the closed curve,
\[
	\oint_{C} A \cdot dr,
\]
is called the circulation of \(A\) around \(C\).
\enlargethispage{0.5cm}
The curl provides a local measure of this tendency to circulate. In the
infinitesimal limit,
\[
	(\nabla \times A) \cdot \hat{n} =
	\lim_{S \to 0} \frac{1}{S}
	\oint_{\partial S} A \cdot dr.
\]
The relation \(B = \nabla \times A\) means that the magnetic field
represents the local circulation density of the vector potential.


The local relation between curl and circulation can be extended to a finite
surface through Stokes' theorem. Let \(S\) be an oriented surface whose
boundary is the closed curve \(C = \partial S\). Then
\[
	\oint_{C} A \cdot dr = \int_S (\nabla \times A) \cdot dS.
\]
The orientation of \(C\) is related to the normal direction of \(S\)
by the right-hand rule.

Using \(B = \nabla \times A\),
\[
	\oint_{C} A \cdot dr = \int_{S} B \cdot dS.
\]
The surface integral on the right-hand side is the magnetic flux
through \(S\),
\[
	\Phi_B \equiv \int_S B \cdot dS = \oint_C A \cdot dr,
\]
so the circulation of the vector potential around a closed curve is
equal to the magnetic flux enclosed by that curve.

\subsection{The electromagnetic potentials}

In electrostatics, the electric field is obtained from the scalar potential
through
\[
	E = - \nabla V.
\]
This relation, however, is not sufficient when the magnetic field varies in
time. In the general electromagnetic case, the electric and magnetic fields
must be described together through the scalar potential \(V\) and the vector
potential \(A\).

Faraday's law states that
\[
	\nabla \times E =
	- \frac{\partial B}{\partial t}.
\]
Using \(B = \nabla \times A\), we obtain
\[
	\nabla \times E = - \frac{\partial}{\partial t} (\nabla \times A) =
	- \nabla \times \frac{\partial A}{\partial t}.
\]
Therefore,
\[
	\nabla \times
	\left(
	E + \frac{\partial A}{\partial t}
	\right) = 0.
\]
A curl-free vector field can locally be expressed as the gradient of a
scalar function. Introducing the scalar potential \(V\), we write
\[
	E + \frac{\partial A}{\partial t} =
	- \nabla V,
\]
and hence
\[
	E = - \nabla V - \frac{\partial A}{\partial t}.
\]
The electromagnetic fields are therefore related to the potentials by
\[
	E = - \nabla V - \frac{\partial A}{\partial t}, \qquad
	B = \nabla \times A.
\]
In the static limit, \(\partial A/ \partial t = 0\), and the familiar
electrostatic relation \(E = - \nabla V\) is recovered.
