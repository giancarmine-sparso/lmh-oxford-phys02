\section{Electromagnetic potentials in quantum mechanics}

Up to this point nothing has forced us to take the potentials seriously. The
classical equations of motion contain only \(E\) and \(B\), and the gauge
freedom
\[
	A \longmapsto A + \nabla \chi, \qquad V \longmapsto V -
	\frac{\partial \chi}{\partial t},
\]
with \(\chi(r,t)\) an arbitrary function, never reaches an observable.
Quantum mechanics removes this comfort.

\subsection{Mathematical interlude II: the quantum Hamiltonian}

In classical mechanics, the motion of a particle is determined by the force
acting on it: Newton's second law fixes the acceleration, and the
trajectory follows by integration.
Quantum mechanics is formulated differently. The state of the particle
is described by a wavefunction \(\psi(r,t)\), whose time evolution is
governed by an operator called the Hamiltonian,
\begin{equation}
	i \hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = \hat H \psi .
	\label{eq:schrodinger}
\end{equation}
For the non-relativistic system considered here, the Hamiltonian
represents the total energy of the particle.



The Hamiltonian is the operator associated with the total energy of the
system. \Cref{eq:schrodinger}
states that the energy operator \emph{generates} the evolution:
the change of \(\psi\) during an infinitesimal interval \(dt\) is
prescribed by \(\hat H \psi\). To specify the dynamics of a quantum system is
therefore to specify its energy, in the same sense in which specifying the
dynamics of a classical particle means specifying the force acting on it.


Classical mechanics admits an equivalent formulation, due to Hamilton, in
which the evolution is generated by the function
\vspace{-5pt}
\[
	H (r, p) = \frac{p^2}{2m}+ U(r), \qquad \dot r =
	\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}, \qquad
	\dot p = - \frac{\partial H}{\partial r},
\]
rather than by \(F\). The trajectories obtained are exactly the Newtonian ones;
what changes is the object taken as primitive. Hamilton's formulation is
written in terms of the potential energy, rather than Newton's, which is
written in terms of forces. In quantum mechanics this choice becomes a choice of
what the theory regards as fundamental.


The simplest consequence of \cref{eq:schrodinger}
fixes the meaning of the Hamiltonian. If \(U\) does not depend on time,
there exist solutions of definite energy,
\vspace{-3pt}
\[
	\psi(r,t) = \psi_E(r)\, e^{-iEt/\hbar},
\]
for which the probability density \(|\psi|^2 = |\psi_E|^2\) is independent of
time. The time evolution of a stationary state consists entirely of a phase
factor. This is the first indication of a structural feature of quantum
mechanics: a global phase, \(\psi \longmapsto e^{i \alpha}\psi\) with
\(\alpha\) constant, leaves every prediction unchanged and is therefore not
an observable. A \emph{relative} phase between two components of a
superposition is a different matter.

\enlargethispage{0.7cm}
If \(\psi = \psi_1 + \psi_2\) then
\[
	|\psi|^2 = |\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2 + 2 \Re \!
	\left(
	\psi_1^* \psi_2
	\right)
\]
and the interference term depends on the phase difference between the two
branches. Quantum mechanics thus provides an observable that has no classical
counterpart, and it is through this observable that the electromagnetic
potentials will enter the physics.


\section{Minimal coupling}

A quick recap of the basics of quantum mechanics is in
order before proceeding.

In quantum mechanics the state of a particle is a function
\(\psi \colon \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{C}\). At every point in space
we have a complex number; this can be visualized as a bizarre clock. The modulus
\(|\psi|\), the length of the hand, is not itself a probability:
its square \(|\psi|^2\) gives the probability density of finding the particle in that precise
spot in space; the argument tells us where the hand is pointing.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\input{figures/clock}
	\caption{Clock representation of a complex wavefunction. At each position,
		the length of the hand gives the modulus \(\abs{\psi(x)}\), while its angle
		gives the phase of \(\psi(x)\).}
	\label{fig:wavefunction-clock}
\end{figure}
\vspace{-11pt}
The modulus is measurable, the direction of the hand is not. If we rotate every hand by the same
angle, \(\psi \longmapsto e^{i\alpha} \psi\), we do not change any measurement.
The absolute phase is just a convention, like choosing the prime meridian.

What is measurable is a difference in phase. If two amplitudes contribute at
the same point, \(\psi_1 + \psi_2\), the squared modulus contains the
cross term \(2 \Re (\psi_1^* \psi_2)\), which depends on the relative angle
between the two hands. When they are aligned, they add; when they point
in opposite directions, they cancel. This is the only way in which
phase becomes observable, and it's the entire mechanism behind interference.

\subsection{Momentum is the hand's rotation rate}
\enlargethispage{0.7cm}
Take the plane wave \(\psi(x) = e^{ikx}\). Its magnitude is constant,
but the hand rotates while advancing in space, at a rate \(k\): \(k\)
radians of phase per unit length. The faster it rotates, the shorter
the wavelength.
This concept is explained by the de Broglie relation; to a short
wavelength corresponds a large momentum
\[
	p = \hbar k.
\]
And we can measure the spatial rate at which the phase rotates
by differentiating:
\[
	-i \hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x} e^{ikx} = \hbar k e^{ikx} = pe^{ikx}.
\]

\subsection{Comparing phases at different points}

When we write \(\nabla \psi\) we are comparing the value of the
wavefunction at \(x\) with its value at a nearby point \(x + dx\).
However, such a comparison requires a convention that defines when they
are to be regarded as equal.

Since the absolute phase of a wavefunction has no physical meaning, there
is no unique, physically preferred phase convention throughout space.
The ordinary gradient \(\nabla\) implicitly assumes that the same
phase convention is being used at every point. This is a choice of gauge,
not an observable fact.

The vector potential \(A\) provides the correction needed to compare
phases defined in different local conventions. Over an infinitesimal
displacement \(dx\), the corresponding phase correction is
\[
	\frac{q}{\hbar} A \cdot dx.
\]
The ordinary gradient therefore contains both the physical variation of the
state and a contribution arising from the chosen local phase convention.
To isolate the gauge-covariant variation, we introduce the covariant
derivative
\[
	D = \nabla - \frac{iq}{\hbar}A.
\]
Multiplying by \(-i \hbar\) we obtain
\[
	-i \hbar D = - i \hbar \nabla - qA.
\]
This is minimal coupling: the ordinary derivative is replaced by a covariant
derivative that compares the wavefunction consistently across different
points.


The operator
\[
	\hat p = -i \hbar \nabla
\]
represents the canonical momentum, whereas
\[
	\hat \pi = \hat p - qA = - i \hbar \nabla - q A
\]
is the kinetic momentum, related to the particle's velocity by \(\hat \pi =
mv\). So in the presence of a vector potential:
\[
	\hat{\pi} = m\hat{v}, \qquad \hat{p} \neq \hat{\pi}.
\]

If the argument of \(\psi\) at a point is only defined relative to some
reference, then nothing stops us from choosing that reference differently
at every point. We can pick any function \(\chi(x)\) and rotate the phase
at \(x\) by the angle \(\frac{q}{\hbar} \chi(x)\):
\[
	\psi \longmapsto e^{iq\chi / \hbar} \psi.
\]
This is a relabelling, not a change of state. The modulus is untouched,
\(|e^{iq \chi / \hbar} \psi|^2 = |\psi|^2\), so every probability is
exactly what it was.

We now recall that \(A\) recorded how much the reference rotates when
we step from \(x\) to \(x + dx\). When re-defining the reference point by
point, we have also changed the rate at which it rotates between
neighbouring points, and that rate changes precisely by the added gradient
\[
	A \longmapsto A + \nabla \chi.
\]
The two modifications are not independent. One is forced by the other,
and together they cancel exactly. Applying \(\nabla\) to the rotated
wavefunction produces an extra term from differentiating the exponential,
\[
	\nabla \left(e^{iq \chi /\hbar}\psi \right) = e^{iq\chi/ \hbar}
	\left(\nabla \psi + \frac{iq}{\hbar}(\nabla \chi)\psi\right),
\]
and the shifted potential \(A + \nabla \chi\) subtracts exactly that extra
term back out. The combination \(-i \hbar \nabla - q A\) therefore survives
unchanged, up to the overall phase factor. No measurement notices anything.


\section{The Schrödinger equation}


We now have every element needed to write the
equation of motion of a charged particle. The quantum
Hamiltonian fixes the dynamics of a quantum system; minimal
coupling tells us that, in the presence of an
electromagnetic field, the operator \(-i \hbar \nabla\) no
longer represents the momentum associated with the motion of
the particle. Putting the two statements together produces
the Schrödinger equation for a particle of charge \(q\) in
an external electromagnetic field.

For a free particle the classical Hamiltonian is the
kinetic energy alone,
\[
	H = \frac{p^2}{2m},
\]
and the canonical quantization prescription \(p \longmapsto
\hat p = -i \hbar \nabla\) gives
\[
	\hat H_0 = - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2.
\]
The kinetic energy is always \(\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\). What changes
in the presence of a field is which operator represents
\(mv\): that role belongs to the kinetic momentum
\[
	\hat \pi = \hat p - qA = -i \hbar \nabla - qA,
\]
and not to the canonical momentum $\hat{p}$. The kinetic term must therefore be built
from $\hat{\pi}$,
\[
	\hat{T} = \frac{\hat{\pi}^{2}}{2m}
	= \frac{1}{2m}\bigl(-i\hbar\nabla - qA\bigr)^{2}.
\]

The scalar potential enters as in the classical case: a charge
\(q\) placed at position \(r\) has electrostatic potential
energy \(U = qV(r)\), which acts on the wavefunction by
multiplication. The Hamiltonian of a charged particle in an
electromagnetic field is therefore
\[
	\hat H = \frac{1}{2m} \bigl(-i\hbar\nabla - qA\bigr)^{2} + qV,
\]
and the Schrödinger equation reads
\[
	i \hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = \hat H \psi =
	\left[\frac{1}{2m} \bigl(-i \hbar \nabla -
		qA\bigr)^2 + qV \right] \psi.
\]
Expanding the square requires some care, because \(\nabla\)
and \(A\) do not commute; the operator \(\nabla\) acts on
both \(A\) and \(\psi\). Applying \(-i \hbar \nabla -qA\)
twice,
\[
	\bigl(-i\hbar\nabla - qA\bigr)^{2}\psi
	= -\hbar^{2}\nabla^{2}\psi
	+ i\hbar q\,(\nabla\cdot A)\,\psi
	+ 2i\hbar q\,A\cdot\nabla\psi
	+ q^{2}A^{2}\psi ,
\]
so that
\[
	i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}
	= \left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}
		+ \frac{i\hbar q}{2m}(\nabla\cdot A)
		+ \frac{i\hbar q}{m}\,A\cdot\nabla
		+ \frac{q^{2}A^{2}}{2m}
		+ qV\right]\psi .
\]
The terms linear in \(A\) are the ones responsible for the effects
we are interested in. The term \(q^2A^2/2m\) is quadratic in the potential
and in the charge, not in the field, and it is not negligible in general:
it is responsible, for instance, for the diamagnetic response of atoms.
Nor does the argument that follows need to drop it: the Aharonov--Bohm phase
is derived from the full operator \((-i\hbar\nabla - qA)^2\) and is exact.
If the gauge
is chosen so that \(\nabla \cdot A = 0\), the two first-order
terms collapse into a single one.

\subsection{Gauge covariance}

The equation just written contains the potentials, not the
field, and the potentials are not unique. We must therefore
check that the physics it describes does not depend on that choice.


Perform a gauge transformation and, at the same time, the local phase rotation
introduced in the previous section:
\[
	A' = A + \nabla\chi, \qquad
	V' = V - \frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}, \qquad
	\psi' = e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\,\psi .
\]
The key property is the one already established for the covariant derivative: the
extra term produced by differentiating the exponential is exactly cancelled by the
extra term in $A'$,
\[
	\bigl(-i\hbar\nabla - qA'\bigr)\psi' = e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\bigl(-i\hbar\nabla - qA\bigr)\psi ,
\]
and, applying the same identity twice,
\[
	\bigl(-i\hbar\nabla - qA'\bigr)^{2}\psi' = e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\bigl(-i\hbar\nabla - qA\bigr)^{2}\psi .
\]

The time derivative produces an extra term of its own,
\[
	i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi'}{\partial t}
	= e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\left(i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}
	- q\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}\,\psi\right),
\]
while the transformed scalar potential contributes
\[
	qV'\psi' = e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\left(qV - q\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}\right)\psi .
\]
The same term $-q\,(\partial\chi/\partial t)\,\psi$ appears on both sides and cancels,
leaving
\[
	i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}
	= \left[\frac{1}{2m}\bigl(-i\hbar\nabla - qA\bigr)^{2} + qV\right]\psi ,
\]
that is, the original equation. The Schrödinger equation is therefore \emph{covariant}
under gauge transformations: it does not leave $\psi$ unchanged, but it retains its
form, and since $|\psi'|^{2} = |\psi|^{2}$ no prediction is affected.

This is a stronger statement than the classical one. Classically, gauge freedom was a
redundancy in the description of $E$ and $B$; here it is inseparably tied to the
freedom of choosing the phase convention of the wavefunction at each point. Fixing a
gauge for $A$ and fixing a local phase convention for $\psi$ are the same act, and
neither of the two changes can be made without the other.

The symmetry between the scalar and the vector potential becomes explicit if we
introduce the covariant derivative in time as well,
\[
	D_{t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \frac{iq}{\hbar}V ,
	\qquad
	D = \nabla - \frac{iq}{\hbar}A ,
\]
so that the equation takes the compact form
\[
	i\hbar D_{t}\psi = \frac{1}{2m}\bigl(-i\hbar D\bigr)^{2}\psi .
\]
The scalar and vector potentials enter through a single prescription, applied to
derivatives in time and in space alike.
